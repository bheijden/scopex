Method

How to run a compile-time comparison that cannot lie to you — which regime you are in, how to measure without fooling yourself, and the eight ways this project fooled itself anyway. The instrument is the easy part; this is the hard part.

Grounded in 126 measured arms across 8 real solvers, a 6-arm adversarial re-measurement, and a sealed-prediction hold-out of 432 timed compiles on 3 programs never previously touched.
Read §3 before you believe any result, including one of your own.
The single most useful thing on this page The best-calibrated prediction of any compile-time intervention is "it will do nothing." That prediction scored 27/33 out of sample and beat every rule built from program structure. Semantics-preserving source rewrites went 0 for 14 as a class (geometric mean 1.019), and 0 of 18 such arms on three hold-out programs produced a shippable win.

Meanwhile the reproducibility floor is 3.9–52% depending on the program. So the default posture is not "which knob?" but "can this measurement even resolve what I am claiming?"

§1Which regime — the one fork that held

This is the one structural fork in this whole body of work that held. It was measured on structurally unrelated programs, and it inverted a prior claim of ours rather than confirming one — which is why it survived where the cost laws did not.

The classifier, three ways, cheapest first

  1. The emitter detector — 5 seconds, no timing at all. 
    t = jax.jit(fn).lower(*a).compile().as_text()
t.count('triton_softmax'); t.count('gemm_fusion_dot'); t.count('__cublas$')
    A zero count makes the matching Triton/GEMM flag a provable no-op — confirmed by a byte-identical optimized module on solver B. Presence is necessary, not sufficient: a transformer with 10 triton_softmax fusions got zero benefit from disabling that rewriter.
  2. --xla_gpu_print_compilation_stats=true — one run. If autotuner is near the top, you are autotune-bound. On a synthetic 60-deep chain it read autotuner, 1, 470.647 of a 533.696 ms total (88%), and adding --xla_gpu_autotune_level=0 dropped the total 268.256 → 28.214 ms (9.5×). Needs TF_CPP_MIN_LOG_LEVEL=0. See §3 for its three traps.
  3. 1 − (leaf-pass-sum ÷ RunHloPasses) ≈ the autotuner's share, from one hlo_pass_pipeline=1 log, module-filtered.

Same story in the other direction. --xla_disable_hlo_passes=triton-softmax-rewriter measured 0.68–0.75× on solver A only, was a provable no-op on solver B (byte-identical module) and on a transformer, and was a regression on a rank-3 physics chain (1.117 / 0.972 / 1.195 against a null of 1.242 / 1.245 / 1.112). --xla_disable_hlo_passes=priority-fusion is 0.67–0.77× on real code and 1.6–2.5× WORSE on a synthetic — the sign flips.

Why the pass-bound regime has no flag win — the mechanism Killing the expensive pass makes it slower. priority-fusion is 57% of HLO-pass leaf time on solver A; disabling it costs 1.164× more total compile (3 pairs 1.16/1.13/1.17), because the unfused module has 4.3× more computations to code-generate — the panel goes 10025/520/527 → 13161/2241/3011. --xla_fuel=priority-fusion=0 reproduces this independently at 1.216× (panel 13002/2236/2932). And --xla_disable_hlo_passes=algsimp is 2.508× SLOWER with panel 36189/681/1227 — the module grows 3.6× because nothing folds it.

Compile time is not the pass budget; it is the pass budget plus what the pass's output costs downstream.

§2Measuring without fooling yourself

This section is the largest surviving output of the whole project. Every item was established, then re-tested, then survived. Violating any of them produces confident nonsense — §7 is the evidence.

1 · The reproducibility floor is model-dependent and LARGE

Six compiles of a byte-identical module, fresh subprocess each, idle box, identical structural panel every time:

modelopt instrmedianspreadmax/min
solver C2,2662.64 s52.0%1.660
solver B4,7224.13 s30.2%1.327
solver A10,0646.20 s3.9%1.040

Resolution improves with program size, because the noise is per-process fixed cost (CUDA context, library load, cold page cache) of ~0.3–1.4 s. Anything under ~3 s of compile is effectively unmeasurable cross-process. Independently, a 12-round complete Latin square with 144 observations and a linear model removing round and position still had five byte-identical arms spanning 1.09–1.15×. Read anything between 0.87 and 1.15 as no result.

Three qualifications, all measured. (a) The floor is not a fixed property of the model — six byte-identical solver A compiles gave 22.7% spread in one session and 14.8% an hour later on the same idle box. Measure the floor in the same session as the arms. (b) The size rule does not hold on CPU: spread went 25.4 → 26.3 → 37.0% over the same three models as they grew. Budget a wider floor on CPU, not a narrower one. (c) In-process repeats are much tighter when the box is quiet — three byte-identical compiles in one process gave 15.468 / 15.820 / 15.579 s, a 2.3% peak-to-peak, because the per-process fixed cost is paid once. That advantage evaporates under load: at load average 17.6 the same design gave base = 14.58 / 36.11 / 9.11 s and a byte-identical base2 = 34.09 / 8.24 / 31.14 s.

2 · Discard round 0

solver C ran 3.45 → 2.98 → 2.28 → 2.46 → 2.64 → 2.08. The 126-arm corpus measured the same thing as a position-0 penalty of 1.03–1.51× across six programs. A design that puts base first in every sweep systematically pays that penalty and flatters every other arm.

3 · Report the MEDIAN of per-round ratios, with the round count

min is a function of the round count, not of the arm. On a byte-identical module, min-of-first-k walks 0.969 → 0.886 → 0.778 as k goes 2 → 3 → 10. On a 12-round Latin square a NULL arm reached 0.4347. Every result this project ever reported as a min is optimistic by an amount that grows with how long the campaign ran.

4 · Carry ≥2 NULL arms, and reference the null centroid — not base

base and a provably-inert null compile a byte-identical module and still differ by up to 14%, in a program-dependent direction: solver A +12.6%, solver D +9.0%, solver E +13.6%, solver B −0.8%, solver C −4.7%, lsmr −8.5%, solver H −7.2%. And band width is systematically underestimated 1.2–1.8× at 3–9 null observations — it is a function of that count, not of the box (1.08×@3 obs → 1.25×@15 → 1.89×@42 on solver C). Restricted-permutation bands are anticonservative: one computed on four of five byte-identical arms put the fifth outside it.

Two good null arms: --xla_disable_hlo_passes=rematerialization (rc 0, byte-identical module on every program tested) and a deliberate typo like priority_fusion. On CPU, --xla_gpu_autotune_level=0 is provably inert and is the correct null.

6 · Interleave and rotate — without reversal

Rotate-then-reverse puts the same arm first twice and biases it slow. Without order-alternation at all, one verifier reproduced a prior design on solver B and got a "win" of 0.576 / 0.734 / 1.081 — while the null arm in the same rounds gave 0.623 / 0.793 / 1.110.

Prefer in-process interleaving via compiler_options. It removes the 0.3–1.4 s per-process fixed cost from the comparison and lets arms alternate round by round:

lo = jax.jit(body).lower(x)                    # trace+lower once per round
c  = lo.compile(compiler_options={"xla_disable_hlo_passes": arm} if arm else None)
# wrap EVERY arm in try/except -- a RET_CHECK kills the whole process otherwise

But Lowered.compile() on the same object does not recompile — solver A round 0 = 16.74 s, rounds 1 and 2 = 0.0001 s. To force N genuine compiles in one process you need a fresh function object each time (a factory returning a new def). Similarly _cached_lowering_to_hlo is a weakref_lru_cache keyed on the ClosedJaxpr, so two independently-traced Traced objects of the same function share one lowered module — any A/B that lowers the same jaxpr twice measures nothing the second time.

8 · Print a structural panel beside every timing

Covered in §3. The one addition here: always pin --xla_gpu_autotune_level=0 when using the panel as a gate, because two flags that cannot touch HLO (--xla_gpu_generate_debug_info, --xla_gpu_disable_gpuasm_optimizations) nevertheless move it — and adding autotune_level=0 collapses them back to the baseline panel exactly, proving the difference was autotuner-mediated. A genuine structural change survives that; an artefact does not.

9 · Parity is max|Δ| / max|A| with the bit-exact count beside it

A sum-over-outputs check reported 7.16e-16 while only 54 of 224 outputs were bit-exact and 81 differed by >1e-6 relative. Check value · grad · vmap(grad) · HVP, and make the gate backend-agnostic. The reason all four are needed: at an implicit-root tier the residual never runs in the forward program, so a wrong derivative leaves the primal bit-identical (1.2e-16 across eleven rows) while gradient error tracks the disagreement with log-log slope exactly 1.0000. A one-character typo in a declared JVP gave VALUE PASS 0.0 and GRAD FAIL 9.57e-1.

10 · One subprocess per model, and assert the environment

Sibling _defs.py files collide in sys.modules: four "different" models returned identical HLO (4818 instructions). Minimum assertions for any harness:

assert not jax.config.jax_compilation_cache_dir       # or one arm loads another's executable
assert jax.devices()[0].platform == "gpu"            # CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES fails SILENTLY
assert not os.environ.get("CUDA_LAUNCH_BLOCKING")      # 2.40x on warm runtime, 1.0x on compile
print(os.getloadavg())                              # record it; a ratio at load 17 is not a result

11 · Prefer CPU-seconds on a shared box, and know when they invert

Four consecutive compiles of the same module under contention: wall spread 2.17×, XLA self-CPU 1.17×, ptxas child-CPU 1.15×. But CPU-seconds hide parallelism changes and invert for parallelism dials (force_compilation_parallelism=1 reads 0.63× CPU and 1.52× wall on the same run). Report both; lead with CPU under load.

The checklist, in priority order
  1. Put a NULL arm in every run — byte-identical to base. If your arm's band overlaps the null's, you measured nothing.
  2. Print the structural panel beside every timing, under autotune_level=0. Deterministic and load-immune.
  3. Assert the persistent cache is off, the backend is what you think, and CUDA_LAUNCH_BLOCKING is unset.
  4. Filter jax.monitoring by fun_name and give your probe a distinctive name.
  5. Discard round 0; median of per-round ratios; quote the round count and the null-observation count. A ratio without those three is uninterpretable.
  6. Record uptime before and after.

§3The failure modes

Roughly 60 confident claims died here, most of them ours. What survives is the failure modes, because the modes recur and you will hit them in your own program. Each carries one or two killing measurements — the full row-by-row ledger has been cut; the lesson never needed it.

The corpus result that frames everything below 126 measured arms across 8 real solvers, a 6-arm adversarial re-measurement, and a sealed-prediction hold-out of 432 timed compiles on 3 models never previously touched.

Nothing computable from the program predicts compile time out of sample. The best structural predictor in the corpus — kmax, Spearman +0.558, n=42 — failed on the hold-out and changed sign: −0.214 / −0.190 / +0.595. Instruction count is −0.011 on the decisive rows: exactly zero. Against the correct null ("every arm lands at this campaign's mean") the best structural predictor buys ~5.8% of skill, and on several campaigns the skill is negative.

By class: 0/20 NULL · 0/14 REWRITE (semantics-preserving source changes, geometric mean 1.019) · 7/41 SPLIT (all seven on one program) · 15/24 REMOVE (ablations that cannot ship) · 4/8 TRAJECT · 3/4 EMITTER (2 of the 3 later refuted). 0 of 18 semantics-preserving arms on 3 hold-out models produced a shippable win.

Mode A · The ceiling mistaken for a lever

An ablation says what REMOVAL is worth. A restructure delivers a fraction of it. Attribution ranks; it does not price.

  • "this ablation is worth 63%, so restructuring is worth ~63%" — restructuring delivered 10–17% on the same program.
  • The most-blamed single line in the program (604 instructions, 8.74%, top-10 in 5 of 17 models) was worth ZERO: 0.937 / 0.939 / 0.952 against a null of 0.912 / 0.993 / 0.967, with instructions growing. On a second model it never beat base and grew instructions +14.9%.

State an ablation as "at most this much is here". Any candidate whose only evidence is an attribution is unpriced, not "worth N%".

Mode B · The deterministic proxy that anti-correlates

Structure is deterministic and seconds are not, which makes structure seductive. It does not follow that it is causal.

Mode F · The regime fallacy — carrying a result across programs, backends, dtypes, shapes or ranks

This is the mode that produced the most retractions, and the one this project keeps re-learning. Five results in this document are device-dependent; assume yours is too until you have run both.

The seven named traps

Every entry above is an instance of one of these. When you feel one, stop.

  1. The Ceiling Mistaken for a Lever. Tell: your only evidence is a stub, an op_name apportionment, or a source-attribution share. Discipline: attribution ranks candidates for ablation; ablation states an upper bound; only a restructure priced against a null arm states a win.
  2. The Deterministic Proxy That Anti-Correlates. Tell: the proxy moved a lot and the clock did not (−37% instructions → 0.613×, −1% → 0.635×), or the proxy moved the "right" way and the clock went the wrong way. Discipline: never price by structure.
  3. The Statistic That Improves With Your Campaign. Tell: your headline number gets better as you add rounds; you have one null arm; your reference is base; you rotate-then-reverse. Discipline: median of per-round ratios, ≥2 nulls in the same rounds, null centroid as reference, rotate without reversal, and quote all three counts.
  4. The Instrument That Bills the Wrong Thing. Tell: an implausible pass wins; your total exceeds the wall clock; a count comes out exactly 0, or comes out identical when it cannot legitimately be. Discipline: scopex.record first; filter every log-derived total to your module; prefer the maintainer-provided route over a parser you wrote.
  5. The Arm That Never Took, Or Took Something Else. Tell: byte-identical structural panels with different seconds. Four ways it happens: the stub patched an object the builder does not use; two "independent" ablations are secretly one (stub a consumer, DCE removes its producer); the stub changed shapes or dtypes too; or the flag name was silently ignored. Discipline: print the panel beside every timing.
  6. The Regime Fallacy. Tell: your evidence is one program or a synthetic; the flag's sign flips; your law's coefficients were fitted at one array shape; you are comparing a CPU number to a GPU number. Discipline: classify first; never carry a pass-disabling result between programs; remember the corpus verdict — the best-calibrated prediction is "nothing will happen."
  7. The Cache That Isn't Yours. Four are in play: JAX's in-memory jit cache, JAX's persistent executable cache (admission threshold 1.0 s), Warp's on-disk module cache, and the CUDA driver's PTX→SASS JIT at ~/.nv/ComputeCache — worth 334×, global, ~1 GiB, evicting. Tell: a "cold" number that improves on the second run of a fresh process; a result that reproduces three times and is still wrong.
